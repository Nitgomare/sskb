    1. Introduction¶

    The Paris Agreement highlights the transition to clean energy as a key strategy for addressing climate change and achieving sustainable development [1]. Harnessing renewable energy sources, such as wind energy, is essential for global sustainability [2]. Countries worldwide are rapidly deploying wind turbines for electricity generation, with large-scale installations both onshore and offshore [3,4]. According to the Global Wind Report 2025 published by the Global Wind Energy Council, the newly installed global wind turbine capacity reached 117 GW in 2024, raising the total installed capacity to 1136 GW, which is 11 % higher than in 2023 [5]. In 2025, new installations are expected to reach 138 GW. From 2025 to 2030, the compound annual growth rate is projected to be 8.8 %, with a cumulative capacity increase of 981 GW by 2030 [5].

    Wind turbines are typically installed in areas with abundant wind resources, such as plateaus, mountainous regions, and coastal zones [6]. However, they face various faults due to factors such as variable wind loads, corrosion from rain and snow, and lightning strikes. If early-stage faults are not detected promptly, they can lead to structural damage, which may escalate into catastrophic accidents [7]. For instance, in 2020, a blade fracture occurred at the V150-4.2 MW wind turbine at the Timber Road IV wind farm in Ohio, USA. In 2023, a lightning strike in Kailu County, Inner Mongolia, China, caused a repaired blade to fracture again, rendering the rotor uncontrollable and resulting in the wind turbine toppling. In 2024, a blade cracking incident occurred at the Märkenkall wind farm in Finland. Since 2020, multiple incidents at wind farms operated by the international wind turbine manufacturer GE in Sweden, Brazil, the USA, and Germany have resulted in significant economic losses and safety risks. Statistics show that prognostics and health management (PHM) for wind turbines accounts for over 30% of total costs [8]. Therefore, PHM is essential for ensuring safety and improving economic performance, necessitating further advancements to optimize the reliability and cost-efficiency of wind turbines.

    Wind turbine faults primarily result from manufacturing defects, harsh environmental conditions, and prolonged use. Manufacturing defects, such as bubbles, voids, or uneven fiber distribution, can compromise the structural integrity of wind turbines. Harsh environmental factors, including sand erosion, ice accumulation, and thunderstorms, can cause wear, cracking, and other forms of damage. Continuous operation over time leads to material degradation and aging, which shortens the wind turbines' service life. Structural damage in wind turbines typically progresses through five stages: initial localized damage, transition to macroscopic deterioration, degradation of mechanical performance, expansion of structural damage, and eventual structural failure [9]. Once structural damage propagates, it can quickly lead to catastrophic failure [10]. Early monitoring, diagnosis, and fault warning are essential to prevent safety incidents and minimize economic losses.

    Several researchers have proposed various methods for wind turbine PHM, including the use of strain signals [11], vibration signals [12], acoustic emission signals [13], ultrasonic signals [14], microphone array signals [15], SCADA data [16], and image data [17]. Although these methods show promising results, they face limitations when applied to wind turbines. One challenge is that many existing methods are not suitable for on-site use by maintenance engineers. Some methods are overly complex and require numerous parameters, which can be difficult for engineers to configure and apply in the field. Additionally, early-stage damage in wind turbine structures is often small, and the underlying fault mechanisms are not fully understood. These damages involve complex mechanical behaviors across multiple physical domains, complicating accurate fault detection with current methods. Moreover, many methods struggle with generalization and fail to detect new or unknown faults [18]. Therefore, developing more effective PHM methods is essential to improve the reliability of intelligent operation and maintenance management for wind turbines.

    Large-scale foundation models (LSF-Models) have demonstrated significant potential across various industries [19-21]. In smart manufacturing, LSF-Models optimize production processes, decode complex alarm signals, analyze equipment failures, and propose actionable solutions [22]. In the power industry, they analyze large volumes of historical data to assess grid conditions and support energy management decisions [23]. Over 70% of operation and maintenance engineers report satisfaction with the analytical results provided by LSF-Models [24]. Additionally, LSF-Models interpret log data, identify root causes of operational incidents, and offer targeted solutions to address failures [25]. Beyond these domains, LSF-Models have achieved breakthroughs in specialized fields, including medical clinical knowledge coding [26], weather forecasting [27], and biological protein structure prediction [28].

    While LSF-Models have been applied across various domains, their use in wind turbine PHM faces significant challenges. A primary issue is that the training data for LSF-Models is primarily sourced from publicly available internet resources, which lack the specialized knowledge necessary for wind turbines [29,30]. As a result, LSF-Models struggle to address wind turbine PHM tasks due to insufficient domain-specific expertise. Moreover, interpreting data specific to wind turbines, such as measurement signals and maintenance documentation, often requires the expertise of seasoned professionals. Finding effective ways to leverage LSF-Models for improving wind turbine PHM remains a critical research challenge. Currently, systematic review analyses on the application of LSF-Models in wind turbine PHM are scarce. Thus, conducting a comprehensive review of existing literature is essential to provide valuable references for future research in this area. This paper makes the following key contributions:

    (1) This study presents a systematic review of wind turbine PHM. It analyzes the fault mechanisms of critical wind turbine components, such as blades, gearboxes, and bearings, conducts a bibliometric analysis, and summarizes existing PHM methods. Additionally, it compiles 15 publicly available wind turbine fault datasets to facilitate in-depth analysis and support future research in this domain.

    (2) The study systematically explores the application scenarios of LSF-Models in wind turbine PHM, including condition monitoring and anomaly detection, fault diagnosis, remaining useful life (RUL) prediction, and maintenance decision-making. Key technologies of LSF-Models are also summarized, focusing on multimodal alignment and fusion, fine-tuning techniques, integration with local knowledge bases, and intelligent agents based on LSF-Models.

    (3) This study analyzes the challenges of applying LSF-Models in wind turbine PHM. It also discusses future research directions, including research on data preprocessing and multimodal fusion, privacy protection of wind turbine data, general foundation models for wind turbine PHM, multi-agent collaborative scheduling, PHM assistant systems, and trust-aware and interpretable LSF-Models.

    This paper systematically reviews the applications, challenges, and future directions of LSF-Models in wind turbine PHM. Section 2 covers the fault mechanisms of key components in wind turbines, PHM methods, and publicly available datasets. Section 3 provides an overview of LSF-Models. Section 4 examines the application of LSF-Models in wind turbine PHM. Section 5 discusses the challenges and future research directions for LSF-Models in wind turbine PHM. The final section presents the conclusions.
